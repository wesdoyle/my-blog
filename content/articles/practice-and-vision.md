A friend recently asked me what qualities I felt made up an ideal engineering team. 
What came to mind were _vision_ and _practice_.

I'd like to explore this response more deeply and look into why these ideas seem so 
important to healthy engineering teams.

Vision and practice are each useful in their own right. But they also seem to co-emerge 
as interdependent qualities, each strengthening the other; too much attention to one without 
the other produces various kinds of dysfunction.

A good vision orients a team with a shared purpose and an understanding of the world from a 
grounded vantage point. That is, it _interprets_ the current state, and thereby makes it 
possible to give thoughtful strategic reasons for sustaining or changing it. Vision requires 
a map, a compass, and a reason for action or non-action. It helps us understand where we're 
going, anchored by a detailed technical understanding of where we are. Vision imbues work with 
meaning (though not the _only_ meaning). It resonates within a stable organization when it aligns 
with a _shared purpose_ for working toward some end. Like practice, a skillful vision isn't static. 
As we embark on any project, we traverse real terrain, and so we must learn to adapt as reality diverges 
from the vision. Over the course of a journey, a vision may become more or less clear. Either way, 
it must survive or be replaced quickly. In my experience, in the absence of a strong vision, 
one or more (sometimes incompatible) visions emerge; as meaning-makers, we seem to instinctively 
fill such voids when they appear. This is why a unifying vision is important -- it aligns and synchronizes 
work toward shared goals. A visionary engineer understands how the systems we care about work today 
and can illustrate and communicate why they should look different tomorrow.

Practice, on the other hand, describes how we work. It pertains to day-to-day execution. 
Though practice is lower-level and more concrete than vision, I've come to believe it deserves 
the same degree of attention, because it describes how we regularly and habitually introduce 
change into the world. It encompasses things like automating deployment pipelines, 
running end-to-end tests, and structuring codebases. While engineering practice is 
concerned with particulars, a _vision for practicing_ also emerges when we attend 
to practice -- embodied by habits like regularly evaluating our deployment practices 
to maximize safety, writing down our ideas and sharing them to build mutual understanding, 
and addressing cognitive debt so we can think and act clearly.

Work without attention to practice leads to accumulating technical and cognitive debt, 
excess production of waste, and atrophy of technical skill. Work without attention to 
vision leads to a dissolution of purpose, an inability to prioritize effectively, and 
a decline in morale. Practice in excess, without vision, can lead to changing the wrong 
things faster. Vision in excess, without practice, can lead to a disconnection from reality.
