I've recently had the opportunity to refresh a knowledge base at work. 
As I sit down to discover what I can learn, and what I can contribute, 
I'm reflecting on the value of reading and writing about the systems we build. 
As we enter an era where we're writing much less software by hand, I suspect that 
our practice of reading and writing _about_ our systems, including the problems 
we're trying to solve, and how we're solving them, is likely waning as well.

In my career, I've worked on three teams that cared deeply about optimizing 
processes for knowledge sharing. I've worked on many others that valued this practice much less.
The teams that did built norms around the practice of reading and writing content by engineers and domain experts. 
Each was different in terms of scale and culture; one, a ~100-person medical device startup. 
The second, a big-tech (FAANG) company, where I subcontracted for several months. 
The third was within a team I led; we spent several years building custom knowledge base software
within a 15-person software agency.

The factors driving the perceived value of knowledge sharing differed at each company.
The medical device startup's work product was highly regulated; producing and maintaining standardized 
documentation was protocol. However, its leadership also leaned heavily into contributing 
to an internal Q&A board, and protected dedicated time for engineers to spend curating knowledge 
base content. New engineers were encouraged to orient themselves to the company's processes through self-directed 
exploration of its knowledge management systems. The big tech company optimized its knowledge management for a globally-distributed team. 
The knowledge base here helped developers easily identify subject matter experts and navigate massive codebases and complex infrastructure. 
My own team, operating as generalists within an agency, often captured knowledge as a means to minimize the amount 
of time it took to onboard our staff to time-sensitive client work, and to share specialized knowledge 
for career growth in areas like web accessibility and machine learning.

In spite of these differences, working on each of these teams taught me the value and challenges of 
reading and writing about what we build. Processes for knowledge sharing within groups emerge from the 
skills, tastes, and discipline of the group, as well as historical context and other external conditions.

Writing takes patience. It's not easy to write well; it's all the more difficult to write well about complex, 
evolving systems for an audience whose questions and curiosity you must broadly anticipate. 
It's also not always pleasant to read docs -- many knowledge bases are limited in scope, and 
littered with stale and incomplete information.

I believe it's more important than ever for engineers to read and write about what we build. 
The process of human learning, reading, and writing is slow, by the standards of typical work culture; 
this slowness is crucial to our wellbeing. 

I hope we see more teams invest time in thoughtful human learning, including reading and writing. 
I believe it is perennially valuable to turn our attention to one thing at a time, for extended periods. 
It's crucial that we listen to and read the thoughts and decision-thinking of those in our respective communities, 
and that we take time to thoughtfully share our own. The practice of thoughtful knowledge sharing builds intuition, 
empathy, and reasonable decision-making skills, and we must not sacrifice these skills at the expense of productivity.
